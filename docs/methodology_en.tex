\section{Data and returns}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Simple and log returns}

\begin{definition}
For a price $P_t$, the simple return and the log return are
\[
R_t=\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}-1,\qquad r_t=\ln\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}=\ln(1+R_t).
\]
\end{definition}

Their aggregation properties are opposite.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
& Cross-sectional aggregation (across assets) & Time aggregation \\
\midrule
Simple $R$ & $R_{p,t}=\sum_i w_iR_{i,t}$ \quad exact & $\prod_t(1+R_t)-1$ \\
Log $r$ & $r_{p,t}\neq\sum_i w_ir_{i,t}$ & $\sum_t r_t$ \quad exact \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:simple}
The simple return of a portfolio is the weighted average of the simple returns of its
assets, $R_{p,t}=w\T R_t$, where $w$ are the value weights at $t-1$. The property does
not hold for log returns.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The portfolio value is $V_t=\sum_i n_iP_{i,t}$ for holdings $n_i$ fixed over the period.
Then
\[
\frac{V_t}{V_{t-1}}=\sum_i\frac{n_iP_{i,t-1}}{V_{t-1}}\cdot\frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,t-1}}
=\sum_i w_i(1+R_{i,t}),
\]
hence $R_{p,t}=w\T R_t$. For log returns, $r_{p,t}=\ln\sum_iw_ie^{r_{i,t}}$, and by strict
concavity of the logarithm (Jensen), $\ln\sum_iw_ie^{r_i}>\sum_iw_ir_i$ as soon as the
$r_i$ are not all equal.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Consequence.} Portfolio construction and backtesting are always done in simple
returns. Log returns are for statistical modelling (more symmetric, additive over
time). Useful relation: $r=\ln(1+R)=R-R^2/2+O(R^3)$, a negligible gap at daily
frequency but not at annual frequency.

\subsection{Time alignment}

In \py{portopt}, $R_t$ dated $t$ is the return from the close of $t-1$ to the close of
$t$. A weight decided with the information available at the close of $t-1$ earns $R_t$.
This alignment is what rules out look-ahead bias in the backtest
(section~\ref{sec:backtest}).

\subsection{Cleaning}

\py{clean_prices} applies three rules.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Limited forward-fill} (\py{ffill_limit=5}): fills exchange holidays when
mixing markets. Beyond that, a gap is genuinely missing data.
\item \textbf{Never back-fill}: \py{bfill} copies a future price into the past, which is
look-ahead bias in disguise.
\item \textbf{Leading NaNs are kept}: an ETF launched in 2012 has NaNs before. The
backtester only invests in it once it has a full in-sample window. Dropping
``incomplete'' assets upfront would keep only the survivors: \emph{survivorship bias}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Adjusted prices}

\py{download_prices(auto_adjust=True)} returns closes adjusted for splits and
dividends, hence \emph{total return} series. On raw prices, every ex-dividend date looks
like a loss: a bond ETF would artificially lose 3 to 4\pct{} a year.

\py{resample_returns}, \py{load_returns}, \py{simulate_returns}.
\end{code}

\section{Estimating the covariance}\label{sec:cov}

Every method except 1/N consumes $\Sigma$. The quality of the allocation is bounded by
the quality of this estimator.

\subsection{Sample covariance}

\[
S=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^T(R_t-\bar R)(R_t-\bar R)\T .
\]
$S$ is unbiased but noisy as soon as $N/T$ is not small. It has $N(N+1)/2$ parameters:
55 for 10 assets, 5{,}050 for 100 assets. With $T=252$, estimating 5{,}050 parameters
from 25{,}200 numbers makes no sense. If $T\le N$, $S$ has rank at most $T-1$: it is
singular and cannot be inverted.

\begin{theorem}[\citealp{marchenko1967}]
If the $R_t$ are iid with covariance $I_N$ and $N,T\to\infty$ with $q=N/T\in(0,1]$, the
empirical distribution of the eigenvalues of $S$ converges to a law supported on
\[
\lambda\in\bigl[(1-\sqrt q)^2,\ (1+\sqrt q)^2\bigr].
\]
\end{theorem}

With $q=0.5$, the eigenvalues spread between $0.09$ and $2.91$ while they are all equal
to 1. \textbf{Large eigenvalues are overestimated, small ones underestimated.} Yet any
method that inverts $\Sigma$ (minimum variance, Markowitz) puts the most weight on the
lowest-variance directions, exactly where the estimate is most wrong: this is the
\emph{error maximization} of \citet{michaud1989}.

\subsection{Ledoit-Wolf}

The idea is to combine $S$ (unbiased, high variance) with a structured target (biased,
zero variance). The optimal bias-variance trade-off has a closed form
\citep{ledoitwolf2004}.

\begin{definition}[Ledoit-Wolf estimator]
Let $S$ be the sample covariance normalized by $T$\footnote{The paper and the code use
the $1/T$ normalization for $S$ in the shrinkage formula; the difference with $1/(T-1)$
is of order $1/T$.} and $\lVert A\rVert^2=\tr(AA\T)/N$ the normalized Frobenius norm.
Let $\mu=\tr(S)/N$ and
\[
d^2=\lVert S-\mu I\rVert^2,\qquad
\bar b^2=\frac{1}{T^2}\sum_{t=1}^T\lVert x_tx_t\T-S\rVert^2,\qquad
\delta^\ast=\frac{\min(\bar b^2,d^2)}{d^2},
\]
where $x_t=R_t-\bar R$. The estimator is $\hat\Sigma=\delta^\ast\mu I+(1-\delta^\ast)S$.
\end{definition}

\textbf{Interpretation.} $d^2$ measures how far $S$ is from the target (dispersion of
its eigenvalues). $\bar b^2$ measures how uncertain $S$ is (sampling variance estimated
from the observations themselves). $\delta^\ast$ is the share of the observed dispersion
attributable to noise: the smaller $T$ relative to $N$, the stronger the shrinkage.

\textbf{Properties.} For $\delta^\ast>0$, $\hat\Sigma$ is positive definite. Eigenvalues
are pulled toward $\mu$ \emph{without changing the eigenvectors}, and the condition
number improves.

\begin{lemma}[fast computation of $\bar b^2$]
With $S=\frac1T\sum_tx_tx_t\T$,
\[
\sum_{t=1}^T\lVert x_tx_t\T-S\rVert_F^2=\sum_{t=1}^T(x_t\T x_t)^2-T\lVert S\rVert_F^2 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\lVert x_tx_t\T-S\rVert_F^2=(x_t\T x_t)^2-2\,x_t\T Sx_t+\lVert S\rVert_F^2$. Moreover
$\sum_tx_t\T Sx_t=\tr\bigl(S\sum_tx_tx_t\T\bigr)=T\tr(S^2)=T\lVert S\rVert_F^2$.
Summing: $\sum_t(x_t\T x_t)^2-2T\lVert S\rVert_F^2+T\lVert S\rVert_F^2$.
\end{proof}

The $\bar b^2$ term therefore costs $O(TN)$ on top of computing $S$, without forming the
$T$ matrices $x_tx_t\T$.

\fig{lw_spectrum.png}{Eigenvalue spectrum, 50 assets and 75 days, true one-factor model.
The sample covariance crushes the small eigenvalues (the last one is more than 10 times
too small); Ledoit-Wolf lifts them and divides the condition number by 5.}

\subsection{EWMA}

\[
\hat\Sigma_t=\sum_{s=0}^{T-1}\omega_s(R_{t-s}-\bar R)(R_{t-s}-\bar R)\T,\qquad
\omega_s\propto\lambda^s,\quad\lambda=2^{-1/h},
\]
with $h$ the half-life (63 days by default), in the spirit of RiskMetrics
\citep{riskmetrics1996}. The effective number of observations
$(\sum\omega_s)^2/\sum\omega_s^2\approx(1+\lambda)/(1-\lambda)$ is about 180 days for
$h=63$: more responsive to regime changes, but noisier.

\begin{tip}
\begin{itemize}
\item The choice of covariance estimator often matters more than the choice between ERC
and HRP.
\item Means are much harder to estimate than covariances. The standard error of an
annualized mean is $\sigma/\sqrt{Y}$ with $Y$ the number of years: with $\sigma=16\pct$
and 10 years, $\pm5\pct$ on a mean of 8\pct. It does not depend on the sampling
frequency. The error on a variance shrinks with the number of observations: moving to
daily data helps. This is the fundamental reason why every method in this document
ignores expected returns.
\item Beyond 50 assets, prefer a factor model $\Sigma=B\Sigma_FB\T+D$ or nonlinear
shrinkage \citep{ledoitwolf2020}.
\end{itemize}
\end{tip}

\py{estimate_cov}. Every estimator accepts

\section{The common building block: risk contributions}\label{sec:rc}

\subsection{Euler decomposition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:euler}
The volatility $\sigma_p(w)=\sqrt{w\T\Sigma w}$ is homogeneous of degree 1 and
\[
\sigma_p(w)=\sum_{i=1}^Nw_i\,\frac{\partial\sigma_p}{\partial w_i}(w),\qquad
\frac{\partial\sigma_p}{\partial w}=\frac{\Sigma w}{\sigma_p}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\sigma_p(\lambda w)=\lambda\sigma_p(w)$ for $\lambda>0$. Differentiating with respect to
$\lambda$ and evaluating at $\lambda=1$ gives $\sum_iw_i\,\partial_i\sigma_p(w)=\sigma_p(w)$
(Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions). The gradient follows from differentiating
$\sqrt{w\T\Sigma w}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
Marginal, total and relative risk contribution of asset $i$:
\[
\mathrm{MRC}_i=\frac{(\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma_p},\qquad
\mathrm{RC}_i=w_i\frac{(\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma_p},\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{RC}_i}{\sigma_p}=\frac{w_i(\Sigma w)_i}{w\T\Sigma w}.
\]
By proposition~\ref{prop:euler}, $\sum_i\mathrm{RC}_i=\sigma_p$ and the relative
contributions sum to 1.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Interpretation}

Since $(\Sigma w)_i=\Cov(R_i,R_p)$,
\[
\mathrm{MRC}_i=\frac{\Cov(R_i,R_p)}{\sigma_p}=\rho_{i,p}\,\sigma_i=\beta_{i,p}\,\sigma_p .
\]
An asset contributes to risk in proportion to its \textbf{beta to the portfolio}, not to
its own volatility. A very volatile asset that is uncorrelated with the portfolio
contributes little; an asset with $\rho_{i,p}<0$ has a negative contribution: it
reduces risk.

\begin{example}
A 60/40 equity/bond portfolio, $\sigma_E=16\pct$, $\sigma_B=6\pct$, $\rho=-0.2$.
\begin{align*}
\sigma_p^2&=0.6^2\cdot0.16^2+0.4^2\cdot0.06^2+2\cdot0.6\cdot0.4\cdot(-0.2)\cdot0.16\cdot0.06\\
&=0.009216+0.000576-0.000922=0.008870,\qquad\sigma_p=9.4\pct,\\
(\Sigma w)_E&=0.6\cdot0.0256+0.4\cdot(-0.00192)=0.014592,\\
\mathrm{RC}_E/\sigma_p&=0.6\cdot0.014592/0.008870=98.7\pct,\qquad
\mathrm{RC}_B/\sigma_p=1.3\pct.
\end{align*}
A 60/40 portfolio in capital is a 99/1 portfolio in risk. This is the historical
motivation for risk parity.
\end{example}

\end{code}

\subsection{Equal weight (1/N)}

\[
w_i=\frac1N .
\]

\textbf{Intuition.} Estimate nothing, so estimate nothing wrong.

\textbf{Theory.} If the $N$ assets share the same volatility $\sigma$ and the same
correlation $\rho$,
\[
\sigma_p^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\bigl(1+(N-1)\rho\bigr)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\rho\sigma^2 :
\]
diversification removes idiosyncratic risk, not common risk.

\textbf{Key result.} \citet{demiguel2009} compare 14 optimization models (Markowitz,
Bayes-Stein, constrained minimum variance, etc.) on 7 datasets: none significantly beats
1/N in out-of-sample Sharpe ratio. The estimation window needed for Markowitz to beat
1/N is around 3{,}000 months for 25 assets. 1/N is the \textbf{minimum benchmark} for
any allocation research.

\textbf{Limitations.} Allocation in capital, not in risk: the most volatile asset
dominates (section~\ref{sec:rc}). The portfolio depends on how the universe is defined:
adding 5 equity ETFs to a universe of 10 changes it radically.

\begin{tip}
The turnover of 1/N is not zero: weights drift with prices and the portfolio must be
rebalanced (38\pct{} a year in the example of section~\ref{sec:results}). A buy-and-hold
1/N is no longer 1/N after six months.
\end{tip}

\subsection{Inverse volatility}

\[
w_i=\frac{\sigma_i^{-k}}{\sum_j\sigma_j^{-k}},\qquad k=1\ \text{(inverse volatility)},\quad k=2\ \text{(inverse variance)}.
\]

\textbf{Intuition.} Give every asset the same volatility budget while ignoring
correlations: $w_i\sigma_i$ is constant. Also called \emph{naive risk parity}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:invvol}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If all correlations are equal ($\rho_{ij}=\rho$ for $i\ne j$), the inverse
volatility portfolio is the ERC portfolio.
\item If $\Sigma$ is diagonal, the inverse variance portfolio is the minimum variance
portfolio.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) With $w_i=c/\sigma_i$:
$(\Sigma w)_i=\sum_j\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j\frac{c}{\sigma_j}=\sigma_i\,c\,\bigl(1+(N-1)\rho\bigr)$,
so $w_i(\Sigma w)_i=c^2\bigl(1+(N-1)\rho\bigr)$ does not depend on $i$.
(ii) The minimum variance portfolio is $w\propto\Sigma^{-1}\one$ and, for a diagonal
$\Sigma$, $\Sigma^{-1}\one=(1/\sigma_i^2)_i$.
\end{proof}

InvVol is thus ERC under the assumption of homogeneous correlations, and inverse
variance is minimum variance under the assumption of zero correlations. The gap between
InvVol and ERC measures \textbf{the information carried by the correlation structure}.

\begin{tip}
With two assets, InvVol and ERC coincide whatever $\rho$: there is only one
correlation, so it is homogeneous.
\end{tip}

\subsection{Risk budgeting}\label{sec:rb}

\textbf{Problem.} Given budgets $b_i>0$ with $\sum_ib_i=1$, find $w\ge0$,
$\one\T w=1$, such that
\[
\frac{w_i(\Sigma w)_i}{w\T\Sigma w}=b_i\qquad\forall i .
\]
This is a nonlinear system; the trick is to recast it as a convex problem.

\begin{theorem}[\citealp{spinu2013,roncalli2013}]\label{thm:spinu}
Let $\Sigma\succ0$ and
\[
y^\ast=\argmin_{y\in\R^N_{++}}\ f(y),\qquad f(y)=\tfrac12\,y\T\Sigma y-\sum_{i=1}^Nb_i\ln y_i .
\]
Then $y^\ast$ exists and is unique, and $w^\ast=y^\ast/\one\T y^\ast$ is the unique
long-only risk budgeting portfolio. Moreover $y^{\ast\top}\Sigma y^\ast=1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Existence.} $f$ is continuous on $\R^N_{++}$. When some $y_i\to0^+$,
$-b_i\ln y_i\to+\infty$; when $\lVert y\rVert\to\infty$,
$\tfrac12y\T\Sigma y\ge\tfrac12\lambda_{\min}\lVert y\rVert^2$ dominates the logarithmic
terms. $f$ is therefore coercive and attains its minimum in the interior of
$\R^N_{++}$.

\emph{Uniqueness.} $\nabla^2f(y)=\Sigma+\diag(b_i/y_i^2)\succ0$: $f$ is strictly convex.

\emph{First-order condition.} $\partial_if(y)=(\Sigma y)_i-b_i/y_i=0$, i.e.
$y_i(\Sigma y)_i=b_i$: the unnormalized risk contributions of $y^\ast$ are exactly the
budgets.

\emph{Normalization.} $y_i(\Sigma y)_i$ is homogeneous of degree 2, so the relative
contributions of $w^\ast=y^\ast/\one\T y^\ast$ equal $b_i/\sum_jb_j=b_i$.

\emph{Uniqueness of the portfolio.} If $w$ is a risk budgeting portfolio, then
$y=w/\sqrt{w\T\Sigma w}$ satisfies $y_i(\Sigma y)_i=b_i$, hence $\nabla f(y)=0$, hence
$y=y^\ast$ by strict convexity.

Finally, summing the first-order conditions gives $y^{\ast\top}\Sigma y^\ast=\sum_ib_i=1$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Algorithm: cyclical coordinate descent}

We minimize $f$ in $y_i$ alone, the other coordinates held fixed \citep{griveau2013}.
The first-order condition in $y_i$ reads
\[
\Sigma_{ii}\,y_i^2+c_i\,y_i-b_i=0,\qquad c_i=\sum_{j\ne i}\Sigma_{ij}\,y_j .
\]
The discriminant $c_i^2+4\Sigma_{ii}b_i$ is positive and the product of the roots
$-b_i/\Sigma_{ii}$ is negative: there is \textbf{exactly one positive root}, which gives
the closed-form update
\[
y_i\leftarrow\frac{-c_i+\sqrt{c_i^2+4\,\Sigma_{ii}\,b_i}}{2\,\Sigma_{ii}} .
\]
Coordinate descent with exact minimization converges to the global optimum for a
strictly convex function made of a smooth term plus a separable term \citep{tseng2001}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Risk budgeting by coordinate descent (\py{RiskParity._ccd})}\label{alg:ccd}
\KwIn{$\Sigma\succ0$, budgets $b$, tolerance $\varepsilon=10^{-10}$}
\KwOut{weights $w$}
$y_i\gets b_i/\sigma_i$ for all $i$; \quad $y\gets y/\sqrt{y\T\Sigma y}$\;
$s\gets\Sigma y$ \Com*[r]{kept up to date, never recomputed}
\Repeat{$\lVert y-y^{\mathrm{old}}\rVert\le\varepsilon\,\lVert y\rVert$}{
  $y^{\mathrm{old}}\gets y$\;
  \For{$i\gets1$ \KwTo $N$}{
    $c\gets s_i-\Sigma_{ii}y_i$\;
    $y_i'\gets\bigl(-c+\sqrt{c^2+4\Sigma_{ii}b_i}\bigr)/(2\Sigma_{ii})$\;
    $s\gets s+\Sigma_{:,i}\,(y_i'-y_i)$; \quad $y_i\gets y_i'$ \Com*[r]{$O(N)$}
  }
}
\KwRet{$w=y/\one\T y$}
\end{algorithm}

The vector $\Sigma y$ is updated incrementally: $O(N)$ per coordinate, $O(N^2)$ per
sweep, and \textbf{no matrix inversion}. The initialization $y\propto b_i/\sigma_i$,
rescaled to $y\T\Sigma y=1$, starts close to the solution; in practice 10 to 50 sweeps
are enough.

\textbf{Using budgets.} Express a view in risk rather than in capital. In the example,
the \enquote{RB bond tilt} strategy doubles the budget of TLT, IEF and LQD: the three
carry 45\pct{} of the risk (15\pct{} each) versus 30\pct{} under ERC.

\begin{tip}
\begin{itemize}
\item Budgets must be strictly positive (log barrier). To exclude an asset, remove it
from the universe.
\item Without the long-only constraint, the system $y_i(\Sigma y)_i=b_i$ has one solution
per orthant, i.e.\ up to $2^{N-1}$ distinct portfolios \citep{bai2016}. The long-only
solution is the only one you want in practice.
\item To target a volatility (say 10\pct), scale $w$ by $0.10/\sigma_p$: relative
contributions are invariant to leverage.
\end{itemize}
\end{tip}

\py{RiskParity(budgets={...})}, method \py{_ccd}, attribute \py{n_iter_} (number of sweeps).
\end{code}

\subsection{Equal Risk Contribution (ERC)}\label{sec:erc}

ERC is risk budgeting with $b_i=1/N$: every asset carries the same share of risk.

\begin{theorem}[\citealp{maillard2010}]\label{thm:erc}
Let $\sMV$ be the volatility of the long-only minimum variance portfolio. Then
\[
\sMV\le\sERC\le\sEW .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The first inequality is immediate: the long-only minimum variance portfolio minimizes
$\sigma_p$ over the whole simplex, which contains $w_{\mathrm{ERC}}$.

For the second, $\sigma_p(w)=\lVert\Sigma^{1/2}w\rVert$ is convex, hence lies above its
tangent at $w_{\mathrm{ERC}}$:
\[
\sigma_p\bigl(\tfrac{\one}{N}\bigr)\ \ge\ \sERC+\nabla\sigma_p(w_{\mathrm{ERC}})\T\Bigl(\tfrac{\one}{N}-w_{\mathrm{ERC}}\Bigr)
=\frac1N\sum_i\partial_i\sigma_p(w_{\mathrm{ERC}}),
\]
since $\nabla\sigma_p(w)\T w=\sigma_p(w)$ (proposition~\ref{prop:euler}). At ERC,
$w_i\,\partial_i\sigma_p=\sERC/N$, so $\partial_i\sigma_p=\sERC/(Nw_i)$ and
\[
\sEW\ \ge\ \frac{\sERC}{N^2}\sum_i\frac1{w_i}\ \ge\ \sERC,
\]
by the arithmetic-harmonic mean inequality: $\sum_i1/w_i\ge N^2/\sum_iw_i=N^2$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Interpretation.} In the parametrization of theorem~\ref{thm:spinu}, $y^\ast$ also
minimizes $\sqrt{y\T\Sigma y}$ subject to $\sum_i\ln y_i\ge c$ (the value of $c$ only
changes the scale of $y^\ast$). ERC is therefore a minimum variance portfolio under a
logarithmic \textbf{diversification constraint}: without the constraint we recover
minimum variance; the constraint prevents any weight from going to zero.

\fig{erc_two_assets.png}{Two assets, $\sigma_1=18\pct$, $\sigma_2=6\pct$, $\rho=-0.2$.
Minimum variance (5.3\pct) puts 14\pct{} of the risk on the risky asset, 1/N (8.9\pct)
puts 95\pct, ERC (5.7\pct) exactly 50\pct. ERC's volatility is barely above the minimum:
a lot of diversification is bought for very little variance.}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:erctangent}
If all assets have the same Sharpe ratio and correlations are homogeneous, ERC is the
tangency (maximum Sharpe) portfolio.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The tangency portfolio is $w\propto\Sigma^{-1}\mu$. With $\mu_i=s\,\sigma_i$ and
$\Sigma=DCD$, $D=\diag(\sigma_i)$, $C=(1-\rho)I+\rho\one\one\T$:
$\Sigma^{-1}\mu=s\,D^{-1}C^{-1}\one$. Since $C\one=(1+(N-1)\rho)\one$,
$C^{-1}\one=\one/(1+(N-1)\rho)$ and $w\propto D^{-1}\one=(1/\sigma_i)_i$, which is ERC
by proposition~\ref{prop:invvol}.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Why ERC works out of sample.} It does not use expected returns, the most poorly
estimated quantity. It does not invert $\Sigma$: weights depend continuously and stably
on $\Sigma$, whereas minimum variance amplifies the noise in small eigenvalues. And
proposition~\ref{prop:erctangent} makes it optimal under a reasonable ``ignorance''
assumption.

\begin{tip}
\begin{itemize}
\item ERC overweights low-volatility assets (bonds). Its historical performance owes a
lot to the 1981--2020 bond bull market. In 2022, stocks and bonds fell together and
levered risk parity funds lost around 20\pct{} or more.
\item An unlevered ERC has low volatility (8\pct{} in the example). Compare it with 1/N
on Sharpe ratio, or at equal volatility.
\end{itemize}
\end{tip}

\subsection{Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP)}\label{sec:hrp}

\textbf{Intuition} \citep{lopezdeprado2016}. Markowitz treats every asset as a potential
substitute for every other (complete graph). But assets have a hierarchical structure:
SPY and IWM are close, so are TLT and IEF, and these two families are far apart. HRP
allocates between families first, then within them.

\subsubsection*{Step 1: distance}

\[
d_{ij}=\sqrt{\tfrac12(1-\rho_{ij})}\in[0,1].
\]

\begin{proposition}
$d$ is a metric.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $z_i$ be the series of asset $i$, demeaned and scaled to unit norm. Then
$\rho_{ij}=z_i\T z_j$ and $\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert^2=2(1-\rho_{ij})$, so
$d_{ij}=\tfrac12\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert$. Non-negativity, symmetry and the triangle
inequality are inherited from the Euclidean norm; $d_{ij}=0$ if and only if
$\rho_{ij}=1$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Step 2: hierarchical clustering}

Repeatedly merge the two closest clusters. The distance between clusters depends on the
\py{linkage}: \py{single} (minimum distance, original paper, prone to chaining),
\py{complete} (maximum, compact clusters), \py{average} (mean, a good compromise),
\py{ward} (minimizes the increase in within-cluster variance).

\subsubsection*{Step 3: quasi-diagonalization}

Reorder the assets following the leaves of the dendrogram. Correlated assets become
neighbours and the reordered covariance matrix concentrates its large entries near the
diagonal.

\fig{hrp_steps.png}{HRP steps. In the arbitrary order (left), the structure is invisible.
After quasi-diagonalization (right), two blocks appear: rates and credit (LQD, TLT, IEF)
at the top, risky assets (VNQ, EEM, EFA, SPY, IWM) at the bottom, with gold and
commodities in between.}

\subsubsection*{Step 4: recursive bisection}

Start from $w=\one$. Each time a cluster is split into two sub-clusters $L$ and $R$:
\[
V_c=\tilde w_c\T\Sigma_c\tilde w_c,\quad
\tilde w_c=\frac{\diag(\Sigma_c)^{-1}}{\one\T\diag(\Sigma_c)^{-1}},\qquad
\alpha=1-\frac{V_L}{V_L+V_R},\quad w_L\leftarrow\alpha\,w_L,\quad w_R\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\,w_R .
\]
$V_c$ is the variance of the inverse variance portfolio of cluster $c$: each
sub-cluster receives a share inversely proportional to its variance. It is an inverse
variance allocation between two super-assets.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Hierarchical Risk Parity (\py{HRP})}\label{alg:hrp}
\KwIn{in-sample returns, \py{linkage}, \py{split}}
\KwOut{weights $w$}
$\Sigma\gets$ estimated covariance; \quad $\rho\gets$ correlation\;
$d_{ij}\gets\sqrt{(1-\rho_{ij})/2}$\;
tree $\gets$ hierarchical clustering of $d$ (condensed form)\;
order $\gets$ leaves of the tree \Com*[r]{quasi-diagonalization}
$w\gets\one$; \quad stack $\gets$ [order]\;
\While{stack not empty}{
  pop a cluster $c$ from the stack\;
  \lIf{$|c|=1$}{continue}
  $(L,R)\gets$ \eIf{\py{split="bisection"}}{two halves of the list $c$}{two children of node $c$}
  $\alpha\gets1-V_L/(V_L+V_R)$\;
  $w_L\gets\alpha\,w_L$; \quad $w_R\gets(1-\alpha)\,w_R$\;
  push $L$ and $R$ onto the stack\;
}
\KwRet{$w$}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{proposition}
(i) No matrix inversion is needed: HRP works even if $\Sigma$ is singular ($T<N$).
(ii) If $N=2$ or if $\Sigma$ is diagonal, HRP is the inverse variance portfolio.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) Only the diagonals of sub-blocks are inverted. (ii) For a diagonal $\Sigma$,
$V_c=1/S_c$ with $S_c=\sum_{i\in c}\sigma_i^{-2}$, so $\alpha=V_R/(V_L+V_R)=S_L/(S_L+S_R)$:
cluster $L$ receives the inverse variance share of its assets. By induction on the
tree, each asset receives $\sigma_i^{-2}/\sum_j\sigma_j^{-2}$. The case $N=2$ is a single
split between two singletons, with $V_c=\sigma_c^2$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Two splitting variants} (\py{split=}). \py{"bisection"} (original paper) cuts the
\emph{ordered list} into two equal halves, ignoring the tree: a natural cluster of 3
assets in a universe of 10 can be cut in two. \py{"dendrogram"} follows the actual nodes
of the dendrogram; it is the recommended variant.

\begin{tip}
\begin{itemize}
\item López de Prado's original code passes the \emph{square} matrix $d$ to
\py{scipy.cluster.hierarchy.linkage}. SciPy then treats each row as an observation and
computes $\tilde d_{ij}=\lVert d_{\cdot,i}-d_{\cdot,j}\rVert_2$, a distance of distances.
Many implementations reproduce this behaviour without knowing it. \py{portopt} passes the
condensed form (\py{squareform}), hence $d$ itself.
\item HRP is unstable over time: a small change in correlation can reorganize the tree
and move a lot of weight. In the example its turnover (120\pct{} a year) is 2.5 times
that of ERC; at 20~bps of costs, that is 24~bps of performance a year. Try
\py{linkage="ward"} or \py{"average"}.
\item HRP concentrates risk in the least volatile cluster (71\pct{} of the risk on TLT,
IEF and LQD in the example). It is a defensive portfolio more than a risk-diversified
one.
\end{itemize}
\end{tip}

\begin{code}
\py{HRP(linkage="single", split="bisection")}; attributes \py{order_}
(quasi-diagonal order) and \py{linkage_matrix_} (to pass to
\py{scipy.cluster.hierarchy.dendrogram}).
\end{code}

\section{Walk-forward backtest}\label{sec:backtest}

\fig{walk_forward.png}{Rolling-window walk-forward backtest. Weights estimated on returns
up to $t-1$ are held over $[t,t+H)$, then the window moves forward by $H$.}

\subsection{Mechanics}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Walk-forward backtest (\py{Backtester.run})}\label{alg:bt}
\KwIn{returns $R_{0..T-1}$, estimator, $L$, $H$, cost $c$}
\KwOut{out-of-sample returns net of costs}
$w^{\mathrm{drift}}\gets0$ \Com*[r]{start in cash}
$t\gets L$\;
\While{$t<T$}{
  window $\gets R_{t-L..t-1}$ (\py{rolling}) or $R_{0..t-1}$ (\py{expanding})\;
  universe $\gets$ assets with no NaN over the window\;
  $w\gets$ copy of the estimator fitted on the window\;
  $\tau\gets\sum_i|w_i-w_i^{\mathrm{drift}}|$\;
  hold $w$ over $R_{t..t+H-1}$ with weight drift; wealth $\times(1-c\,\tau)$ at $t$\;
  $w^{\mathrm{drift}}\gets$ drifted weights at period end; \quad $t\gets t+H$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

Weights use information up to $t-1$ and earn $R_t$: no look-ahead bias. A unit test
checks this with a spy estimator that records the last date it sees. The estimator is
copied at every date: no state leaks from one window to the next.

\subsection{Weight drift}

Between two rebalancings, a real portfolio does not keep constant weights: they drift
with prices. For $k=1,\dots,H$,
\[
V_k=\sum_iw_i\prod_{s=1}^k(1+R_{i,s}),\qquad
R_{p,k}=\frac{V_k}{V_{k-1}}-1,\qquad
w_i^{\mathrm{drift}}=\frac{w_i\prod_{s=1}^H(1+R_{i,s})}{V_H}.
\]
The implementation is vectorized: \py{np.cumprod(1 + R, axis=0) @ w} gives the whole
path $V_1,\dots,V_H$ at once. With \py{drift=False}, the portfolio is assumed to be
rebalanced to target every day, for free: more optimistic than reality.

\subsection{Transaction costs}

At each rebalancing, one-way turnover is measured against the \textbf{drifted weights}:
\[
\tau_t=\sum_i\bigl|w_i^{\mathrm{target}}-w_i^{\mathrm{drift}}\bigr| .
\]
Measuring against the previous target is a common mistake: if equities rose 10\pct{}
during the month, an ERC portfolio must sell some even if its target did not move.
Ignoring drift understates turnover.

Wealth is multiplied by $(1-c\,\tau_t)$ at rebalancing, with the cost $c$ given in
basis points through \py{transaction_cost_bps}. The first rebalancing starts from cash ($\tau=1$).
Annualized turnover, excluding the initial build-up, is
\[
\mathrm{Turnover}_{\mathrm{ann}}=\frac{\sum_{t>t_0}\tau_t}{\text{number of years}},
\]
and the annual cost is about $c\times\mathrm{Turnover}_{\mathrm{ann}}$: 120\pct{}
turnover at 10~bps costs 12~bps a year.

\begin{tip}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$(L,H)$ is a hyperparameter.} Test a grid and look at the stability of the
ranking of methods, never at the best pair: otherwise you are overfitting the backtest.
\item \textbf{Timing luck.} Shifting the first rebalancing date by $0$ to $H-1$ days gives
$H$ different paths for the same strategy. Their dispersion measures how much of the
result is luck; for a monthly strategy it is often of the same order as the gaps
between methods.
\item \py{window="expanding"} reduces estimation noise but reacts slowly to regimes. A
rolling window of 252 to 756 days is the standard.
\end{itemize}
\end{tip}

\begin{code}
\py{Backtester(returns, estimator, in_sample, out_of_sample, window, transaction_cost_bps, drift)};
\py{.run()} returns the net out-of-sample returns; attributes \py{weights_},
\py{turnover_}, \py{gross_returns_}; methods \py{annualized_turnover()} and
\py{risk_contributions()}. \py{run_backtests(returns, {name: est}, ...)} compares
several estimators on the same grid.
\end{code}

\section{Performance metrics}\label{sec:perf}

Every metric takes a \py{pd.Series} (one strategy) or a \py{pd.DataFrame} (one column
per strategy) of periodic simple returns. \py{summary()} produces the full table.

\subsection{Total return and CAGR}

\[
\mathrm{TR}=\prod_{t=1}^T(1+R_t)-1,\qquad\CAGR=(1+\mathrm{TR})^{P/T}-1 .
\]
The CAGR is the constant rate that yields the same final wealth: it is the return the
investor actually experiences. The annualized arithmetic mean is
$\mu_{\mathrm{ann}}=P\,\bar R$.

\subsection{Volatility drag}

\begin{proposition}
The log growth rate $g=\ln(1+\CAGR)$ satisfies, to second order,
\[
g\approx\mu_{\mathrm{ann}}-\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{ann}}^2}{2}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$g=\frac PT\sum_t\ln(1+R_t)$. With $\ln(1+R)=R-R^2/2+O(R^3)$ and
$\frac1T\sum_tR_t^2=\hat\sigma^2+\bar R^2\approx\hat\sigma^2$ for daily data,
$g\approx P\bar R-P\hat\sigma^2/2=\mu_{\mathrm{ann}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{ann}}^2/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
Two strategies with $\mu_{\mathrm{ann}}=8\pct$: at $\sigma=10\pct$, $g\approx7.5\pct$;
at $\sigma=30\pct$, $g\approx3.5\pct$. In the example of section~\ref{sec:results}, 1/N
has $\mu_{\mathrm{ann}}=7.36\pct$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{ann}}=11.42\pct$: the formula
predicts $g=7.36\pct-0.65\pct=6.71\pct$, the realized value is $\ln(1.0693)=6.70\pct$.
\end{example}

\begin{tip}
This is the strongest argument for variance reduction: at equal mean, lower volatility
means higher final wealth. The multi-asset version is the \emph{rebalancing premium} or
\emph{excess growth rate} \citep{fernholz1982,boothfama1992}: for a constant-weight
portfolio, $g_p-\sum_iw_ig_i\approx\frac12\bigl(\sum_iw_i\sigma_i^2-\sigma_p^2\bigr)\ge0$.
\end{tip}

\subsection{Sharpe ratio}

\[
\SR=\sqrt P\,\frac{\overline{R-r_f}}{\hat\sigma},\qquad
r_{f,\text{period}}=(1+r_{f,\text{ann}})^{1/P}-1 .
\]
Excess return per unit of total risk. It is \textbf{invariant to leverage}: levering
twice doubles both the excess return and the volatility. A mean-variance investor
picks the highest-Sharpe strategy and then sets the leverage.

\textbf{Annualization.} The $\sqrt P$ factor is only valid for iid returns. For AR(1)
returns with autocorrelation $\rho_1$ and a long horizon of $q$ periods \citep{lo2002},
\[
\SR_q\approx\SR_1\cdot\sqrt q\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho_1}{1+\rho_1}} .
\]
Smoothed returns ($\rho_1>0$: illiquid hedge funds, private equity) have an
overstated $\sqrt P$-annualized Sharpe ratio.

\subsection{Sortino ratio}

\[
\mathrm{Sortino}=\sqrt P\,\frac{\overline{R-\tau}}{DD_\tau},\qquad
DD_\tau=\sqrt{\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\min(R_t-\tau,0)^2} .
\]
Only downside volatility is penalized. The sum in the denominator runs over \emph{all}
observations (positive ones count as zero): dividing by the number of negative
observations is a common mistake that inflates the downside deviation. For a symmetric
distribution with a mean small relative to $\sigma$, $DD_0\approx\sigma/\sqrt2$, so
$\mathrm{Sortino}\approx\sqrt2\,\SR$; a ratio well above 1.41 signals positive
skewness.

\subsection{Calmar ratio}

\[
\mathrm{Calmar}=\frac{\CAGR}{|\MaxDD|} .
\]
The number of years of return needed to make up for the worst loss. It depends heavily
on the length of the history (MaxDD can only get worse as $T$ grows): only compare
strategies over the same period.

\subsection{Hit ratio}

\[
\mathrm{Hit}=\frac1T\sum_t\mathbb 1\{R_t>0\} .
\]
Close to 50--55\pct{} for daily allocation strategies, and not very informative on its
own: a 40\pct{} hit ratio with gains twice as large as losses is excellent (trend
following), a 90\pct{} hit ratio can hide a short volatility position (frequent small
gains, rare and huge losses).

\py{sharpe_ratio}, \py{sortino_ratio}, \py{calmar_ratio}, \py{hit_ratio}, \py{summary}.
\end{code}

\section{Risk metrics}\label{sec:risk}

\subsection{Annualized volatility}

$\sigma_{\mathrm{ann}}=\hat\sigma\sqrt P$. A symmetric measure: a 5\pct{} gain ``costs'' as
much as a 5\pct{} loss. It is insufficient for skewed or fat-tailed distributions
(section~\ref{sec:moments}).

\fig{report_03_rolling_vol.png}{252-day rolling volatility. Realized volatility clusters
in regimes: the simulated stress episodes double the volatility of every strategy, but
the gap between strategies stays stable.}

\subsection{Drawdown}

\begin{definition}
With wealth $W_t=\prod_{s\le t}(1+R_s)$ and $W_0=1$,
\[
DD_t=\frac{W_t}{\max\bigl(1,\max_{s\le t}W_s\bigr)}-1,\qquad\MaxDD=\min_tDD_t .
\]
The maximum time under water is the longest consecutive stretch with $DD_t<0$.
\end{definition}

Initial capital counts as a peak: a strategy that loses 10\pct{} on day one has a
drawdown of $-10\pct$, even if $W$ never went above $0.9$.

\fig{drawdown_anatomy.png}{Anatomy of a drawdown: peak, trough, recovery and time under
water (1/N portfolio, simulated data).}

\textbf{Asymmetry of losses.} Recovering a loss $\ell$ requires a gain $\ell/(1-\ell)$:
$-20\pct$ requires $+25\pct$, $-50\pct$ requires $+100\pct$.

\begin{tip}
For a Brownian motion with drift $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma$ \citep{magdonismail2004},
$\mathbb E[\MaxDD]=\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\sigma\sqrt T$ if $\mu=0$, and
$\mathbb E[\MaxDD]\sim\frac{\sigma^2}{2\mu}\ln T$ if $\mu>0$. MaxDD therefore depends
mechanically on the length of the backtest: $-15\pct$ over 5 years is not comparable to
$-15\pct$ over 20 years.
\end{tip}

\fig{report_02_drawdowns.png}{Drawdowns of the six strategies in the example; the dot
marks the maximum drawdown.}

\subsection{Historical VaR and CVaR}

\begin{definition}
With $q_\alpha(R)$ the empirical quantile of order $\alpha$ (5\pct{} by default),
\[
\VaR_\alpha=-q_\alpha(R),\qquad\CVaR_\alpha=-\mathbb E\bigl[R\mid R\le q_\alpha(R)\bigr],
\]
both expressed as a positive loss.
\end{definition}

The 95\pct{} VaR says: ``on 95\pct{} of days, we lose no more than this'', and says
nothing about the other 5\pct. CVaR (expected shortfall) says: ``on the worst 5\pct{} of
days, we lose this on average''.

\fig{var_cvar.png}{Distribution of daily returns of the 1/N portfolio. The normal
distribution with the same mean and standard deviation underestimates the left tail
(shaded area).}

\begin{proposition}
VaR is not subadditive; CVaR is a coherent risk measure \citep{artzner1999}: monotone,
translation invariant, positively homogeneous and subadditive.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Counterexample to subadditivity]
Two independent bonds, each with a 4\pct{} default probability (loss 100, otherwise 0).
For each, $\VaR_{95\%}=0$ since default lies beyond the 5\pct{} quantile. For the
portfolio of both, losses are 0, 100 and 200 with probabilities $92.16\pct$, $7.68\pct$
and $0.16\pct$: $\VaR_{95\%}=100>0+0$. VaR penalizes diversification. On the CVaR side,
each bond has $\CVaR_{95\%}=(4\pct\times100)/5\pct=80$, and the portfolio
$(0.16\pct\times200+4.84\pct\times100)/5\pct=103.2\le160$: subadditivity holds.
\end{example}

The FRTB framework (Basel III) replaced 99\pct{} VaR with 97.5\pct{} expected shortfall
for market risk.

\begin{tip}
Gaussian parametric VaR $-(\mu+z_\alpha\sigma)$ systematically underestimates tail risk.
To stay parametric, use the Cornish-Fisher expansion, which corrects the quantile for
skewness and kurtosis.
\end{tip}

\subsection{Skewness and kurtosis}\label{sec:moments}

\[
\gamma_3=\frac{\mathbb E[(R-\mu)^3]}{\sigma^3},\qquad
\gamma_4=\frac{\mathbb E[(R-\mu)^4]}{\sigma^4},\qquad
\text{excess kurtosis}=\gamma_4-3 .
\]
$\gamma_3<0$ signals a fat left tail (rare but large losses: carry, option selling,
credit). $\gamma_4-3>0$ (leptokurtic) signals more quiet days and more extreme days than
a Gaussian; at daily frequency, 5 to 20 is common for equity indices.

\begin{tip}
These higher moments are very poorly estimated: a single extreme day can flip skewness
from $-1$ to $+1$. Look at them over subperiods before drawing any conclusion.
\end{tip}

Defined in section~\ref{sec:backtest}. It is an implementation risk metric: high
turnover means exposure to costs, market impact and limited capacity.

\section{Statistical significance}\label{sec:stats}

A backtest is one realization of a random process. Before concluding that one strategy
beats another, check that the gap can be told apart from noise.

\subsection{Standard error of the Sharpe ratio}

For iid Gaussian returns \citep{lo2002}, in annual units over $Y$ years,
\[
\se(\SRh)\approx\sqrt{\frac{1+\tfrac12\SR_{\text{period}}^2}{Y}}\approx\frac1{\sqrt Y}.
\]

\fig{sharpe_ci.png}{95\pct{} confidence band of the estimated Sharpe ratio as a function
of track record length.}

For a Sharpe ratio of 0.5 to be significantly positive at 95\pct, one needs
$Y\ge(1.96/0.5)^2\approx15$ years; for a Sharpe ratio of 1, about 4 years. Most 10-year
backtests cannot tell a Sharpe ratio of 0.5 from zero.

\begin{example}[Consequence for the example]
Sharpe ratios range from 0.64 (1/N) to 0.88 (HRP) over 14 years, with a standard error
of about $1/\sqrt{14}=0.27$. The 0.24 gap is less than one standard error: we cannot
claim that HRP beats 1/N. The test on the Sharpe difference between two correlated
strategies \citep{jobsonkorkie1981,memmel2003} has a smaller standard error, but of the
same order here.
\end{example}

\subsection{Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio}

\begin{definition}[\citealp{bailey2012}]
\[
\mathrm{PSR}(\SR^\ast)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{(\SRh-\SR^\ast)\sqrt{T-1}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma_3\,\SRh+\frac{\gamma_4-1}{4}\,\SRh^2}}\right),
\]
where $\SRh$ and $\SR^\ast$ are expressed \emph{per period} (not annualized), $\gamma_3$
is the skewness and $\gamma_4$ the (non-excess) kurtosis. It is the probability that the
true Sharpe ratio exceeds the threshold $\SR^\ast$.
\end{definition}

\textbf{Reading the denominator.} Negative skewness or high kurtosis increase the
variance of $\SRh$, and therefore lower the PSR. A short volatility strategy with a nice
Sharpe ratio but $\gamma_3=-3$ will have a much lower PSR than a trend following
strategy with the same Sharpe ratio and $\gamma_3>0$. \py{summary()} reports
PSR(SR~>~0); above 0.95 the Sharpe ratio is significantly positive.

\subsection{Deflated Sharpe Ratio}

If $K$ variants (parameters, universes, methods) were tested and the best one kept, its
Sharpe ratio is biased upward: even if all true Sharpe ratios are zero, the maximum of
$K$ estimated ones is positive. The DSR \citep{bailey2014} replaces $\SR^\ast$ in the
PSR with the expected maximum of $K$ zero Sharpe ratios:
\[
\SR^\ast_K\approx\sqrt{\Var(\SRh_k)}\left[(1-\gamma_E)\,\Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(1-\frac1K\Bigr)+\gamma_E\,\Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(1-\frac1{Ke}\Bigr)\right],
\]
with $\gamma_E\approx0.5772$ the Euler-Mascheroni constant and $\Var(\SRh_k)$ the
variance of the Sharpe ratios observed across the $K$ trials. With $K=100$, the bracket
equals $0.4228\times2.326+0.5772\times2.680\approx2.53$: an observed Sharpe ratio of
about $2.5$ standard errors is needed to rule out pure data mining.

\begin{tip}
Record the number of variants you tested. It is as important a piece of the backtest as
the Sharpe ratio itself.
\end{tip}

given annualized and converted internally.
\end{code}

\section{Expert checklist}\label{sec:checklist}

\newlist{checklist}{itemize}{1}
\setlist[checklist]{label=$\square$,leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
\textbf{\sffamily Data}
\begin{checklist}
\item Adjusted prices (total return), never returns on raw prices.
\item No back-fill, no dropping of assets with a short history.
\item Simple returns for the portfolio.
\end{checklist}

\textbf{\sffamily Estimation}
\begin{checklist}
\item $N/T$ ratio checked; above 0.1, shrinkage or a factor model.
\item Expected returns never used without a strong prior or heavy shrinkage.
\end{checklist}

\textbf{\sffamily Backtest}
\begin{checklist}
\item Weights computed with information up to $t-1$ only.
\item Weight drift modelled, turnover measured against drifted weights.
\item Realistic costs (5 to 20~bps on liquid ETFs, more on small caps and emerging
markets).
\item Robustness tested on an $(L,H)$ grid and on rebalancing offsets.
\item 1/N benchmark always included.
\end{checklist}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
\textbf{\sffamily Evaluation}
\begin{checklist}
\item Comparison on Sharpe ratio, or at equal volatility, not on raw CAGR.
\item Standard error of the Sharpe ratio computed before any conclusion.
\item PSR for non-normality, DSR if several variants were tested.
\item Drawdowns and CVaR examined, not just volatility.
\item Risk contributions inspected: know where the risk is.
\item Subperiods and regimes analysed: overall performance may come from a single
episode.
\end{checklist}
\end{minipage}
